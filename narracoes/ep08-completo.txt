A $100 Bill Costs Pennies. Who Keeps the Rest?

<break time="1.0s" />

For 2027, the Federal Reserve has ordered up to one point three six billion brand-new hundred-dollar bills.
Face value? Up to about one hundred thirty-seven billion dollars.

<break time="1.0s" />

The cost of the paper, the ink and the labor for each one? About eleven cents.

<break time="1.0s" />

So between the eleven cents it costs and the hundred dollars it's worth, there are ninety-nine dollars and eighty-nine cents. Someone keeps that difference. Right?
Not exactly. The real answer is stranger, and it involves the whole world, a lot of interest, and a few hundred billion dollars that haven't been paid.
This is Billions and Bulls. Let's follow the money.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 1 – The World's Favorite Bill]
Start with a surprise. Cards are everywhere. Phones pay for coffee. And yet, there are more hundred-dollar bills out there than one-dollar bills.

<break time="1.0s" />

According to the Federal Reserve, at the end of 2025 there were about nineteen point nine billion hundred-dollar notes in circulation. And about fifteen point two billion one-dollar notes.

<break time="1.0s" />

In value, it isn't close. Of the roughly two point four trillion dollars in US cash, almost two trillion is in hundreds. More than four out of every five dollars.
And the pile keeps growing. In 2010, there were about seven billion hundreds. Fifteen years later, almost three times as many.

<break time="1.0s" />

Hundreds also live a long time. The Fed estimates that a five-dollar bill lasts less than six years before it's worn out and destroyed.
A hundred-dollar bill? About twenty-four years.

<break time="1.0s" />

That tells you how people use them. A five gets spent. It goes from wallet to register to wallet again.
A hundred mostly sits. In a safe, under a mattress, in a bank box. Or in another country entirely.

<break time="1.0s" />

The Federal Reserve's own staff estimate that more than one trillion dollars in US banknotes were held outside the country in early 2025. Roughly half of all the dollar bills that exist.
For hundreds alone, a Fed working paper estimated that about seventy percent were held abroad at the end of 2022. Nobody knows the exact number. Once a bill leaves the country, it can't be tracked.
In places where the local currency can lose its value fast, a hundred-dollar bill is a way to keep your savings safe. It doesn't need a bank. It doesn't need a phone. It just needs people to trust it.

<break time="1.0s" />

The US used to print even bigger bills. Five hundred, one thousand, five thousand, even ten thousand dollars. They were last printed in 1945, and the government stopped issuing them in 1969, for lack of use.
Today, the hundred is the biggest bill the Fed issues. So, who makes it?

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 2 – Eleven Cents]
US paper money is printed by the Bureau of Engraving and Printing, part of the Treasury, at two plants. One in Washington, and one in Fort Worth, Texas.

<break time="1.0s" />

But the printer doesn't decide how much to print. Every year, the Federal Reserve estimates how much cash people will need, at home and abroad, and places an order. Then it pays the printer for the cost.
For 2027, that order is between four point eight and five point seven billion notes, of every kind. More than a billion of them are hundreds.

<break time="1.0s" />

The Fed publishes the price list. In its latest one, for 2025, the variable cost of printing a one-dollar bill was about four cents. A twenty, about seven cents. And a hundred, eleven point three cents.
The hundred costs more because it carries more security. A blue three-D ribbon woven into the paper, color-shifting ink, a hidden portrait you can only see against the light.
Benjamin Franklin is on the front. The current design went into circulation in October 2013.

<break time="1.0s" />

Those prices don't include everything. On top of the variable costs, the Fed's 2025 budget had almost seven hundred million dollars for the fixed costs. The factories, the engraving, the research.
Even counting all of that, the Fed's whole currency budget for 2025 was about one billion dollars. For a stock of cash worth about two point four trillion.

<break time="1.0s" />

Compare that with coins. Coins are made by a different part of the Treasury, the US Mint. And for one coin, the math went the other way.
In 2024, each penny cost about three point seven cents to make. The Mint says that meant a loss of around eighty-five million dollars in a single year.
On November 12, 2025, the Mint struck its last penny for circulation.

<break time="1.0s" />

The penny lost money every time it was made. The hundred-dollar bill looks like the opposite. Eleven cents in, a hundred dollars out.
So surely, someone is pocketing ninety-nine dollars and eighty-nine cents. Let's find them.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 3 – Nobody Keeps $99.89]
Here's the twist. When a new hundred-dollar bill leaves the Fed, nobody gets ninety-nine dollars and eighty-nine cents. Not the Fed, not the printer, not the government.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here's how cash really gets to you. Your bank needs more bills for its ATMs. It asks the Federal Reserve for them.
The Fed sends the cash, and takes the same amount out of the bank's account at the Fed. One hundred dollars of cash out, one hundred dollars of account balance gone.
The bank didn't get anything for free. It swapped one kind of money for another.

<break time="1.0s" />

On the Fed's books, that bill is not an asset. It's a liability. It's basically an IOU from the Federal Reserve that you can carry around in your wallet.
And it has one very special feature. It pays no interest. Ever.

<break time="1.0s" />

Think about your own cash. Say you keep ten hundred-dollar bills in a drawer for a year. If a savings account pays three percent, you just gave up about thirty dollars of interest.
You didn't lose the thousand dollars. But somebody else earned those thirty dollars. Who?

<break time="1.0s" />

Now look at the other side of the Fed's books. To balance all that cash, the Fed holds assets. Mostly US government bonds. And those bonds do pay interest.

<break time="1.0s" />

So here's the trick. The Fed owes you a piece of paper that pays nothing. And it holds a bond that pays something. The gap between the two is the real profit from printing money.
Economists call it seigniorage. The word comes from the old French for lord, the seigneur, who used to keep a cut when he minted coins.

<break time="1.0s" />

How big can it get? Some simple math, just to get a feel for it. Two point four trillion dollars of cash. If the bonds behind it earn three percent a year, that's about seventy billion dollars a year.
Not from printing. From the interest that cash holders give up, every single day.

<break time="1.0s" />

If you're enjoying the story so far, subscribing helps the channel a lot. Because now we follow that interest, and see who gets it.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 4 – Where the Profit Goes]
The Federal Reserve isn't a normal company. By law, after it pays its costs and a small dividend to its member banks, the rest of its profit goes to the US Treasury.

<break time="1.0s" />

In good years, that's a lot of money. In 2021, the Fed sent the Treasury one hundred seven point four billion dollars.
That same year, producing, issuing and retiring all of the country's paper money cost the Reserve Banks about one billion dollars. Roughly one percent of what they sent to the Treasury.
Not all of that came from cash, of course. The Fed also earns interest on bonds it bought with bank reserves. But cash is the part that has always cost the Fed nothing.

<break time="1.0s" />

So the first answer to our question is the US government. And through the government, American taxpayers. Every dollar the Fed sends to the Treasury is a dollar the government doesn't have to raise in taxes or borrow.

<break time="1.0s" />

But follow the bills one more step. Remember the trillion dollars of US cash held abroad?
Every person outside the US who keeps savings in hundred-dollar bills is, in effect, lending money to the United States. At zero interest. With no end date.
It's one of the quieter advantages of having the world's favorite currency.

<break time="1.0s" />

The Fed's own staff noticed something about that, too. They say foreign demand for dollar bills has slowed in the last couple of years.
One reason, they say, is higher interest rates. When a bank account pays real interest, keeping a stack of bills in a drawer costs you more.
And there's a new competitor. The Fed's staff say digital tokens tied to the dollar, called stablecoins, seem to be replacing paper dollars for some people in developing countries.

<break time="1.0s" />

And that's the hidden price of holding cash. You give up the interest, and inflation slowly eats away at what the bill can buy.
Someone else earns that interest instead. For most of the last century, that someone was the Fed, and then the Treasury.
Until it suddenly wasn't.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 5 – When Printing Money Lost Money]
In 2022, inflation surged, and the Fed raised interest rates fast. That changed the math completely.

<break time="1.0s" />

Remember, the Fed doesn't only owe cash. It also owes banks the reserves sitting in their accounts at the Fed. And since 2008, the Fed has paid interest on those reserves.
During the pandemic, the Fed bought trillions of dollars of bonds, paying for them with new reserves. By 2022, its balance sheet was close to nine trillion dollars. Almost four times all the US cash in circulation.
When rates were near zero, that was cheap. When rates went above five percent, it became very expensive.

<break time="1.0s" />

Meanwhile, many of the bonds the Fed owned had been bought when rates were low. They kept paying low interest.
So the Fed was paying more on what it owed than it was earning on what it owned.

<break time="1.0s" />

In September 2022, most of the regional Federal Reserve Banks stopped sending money to the Treasury. Instead, they started recording what's called a deferred asset. It's basically a running total of the losses they have to earn back before payments can start again.

<break time="1.0s" />

By June 2026, that total was about two hundred thirty-five billion dollars. The Fed says it doesn't affect how it runs monetary policy, and it's not part of the national debt.
But it means the profit from all that cash, the two trillion dollars of interest-free money, is going to fill a hole first. Some of the regional Fed banks have started paying the Treasury again. Most have not.

<break time="1.0s" />

It's not just an American story. In Germany, the Bundesbank reported a loss of nineteen point two billion euros for 2024. Its first loss since 1979. In 2025, it lost another eight point six billion.
For years, the German government had reportedly counted on about two and a half billion euros a year from its central bank. That money stopped arriving.
In 2019, the euro area also stopped issuing its biggest note, the five-hundred-euro bill. The European Central Bank said it was concerned the note could make illegal activity easier.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 6 – So, Who Keeps the Rest?]
So let's follow the hundred-dollar bill one last time.

<break time="1.0s" />

It costs about eleven cents in paper, ink and labor. Nobody pockets the other ninety-nine dollars and eighty-nine cents on the day it's printed.
The money comes later, slowly, as interest. The Fed holds a bond that pays interest, while you hold a bill that doesn't. In normal times, that profit goes to the US Treasury.

<break time="1.0s" />

And who pays for it? Everyone who holds cash. People here, and maybe more than a trillion dollars' worth of savers abroad, giving up interest to keep money they can touch.
Right now, that profit is quietly paying back the Fed's losses before the Treasury sees much of it again.

<break time="1.0s" />

Not everyone thinks big bills are a good idea. The Harvard economist Kenneth Rogoff has argued that large notes like the hundred make tax evasion and crime easier, and that they should be phased out.
Others say cash protects privacy, works when the power goes out, and is a lifeline for people without a bank, or in countries where the bank can't be trusted.

<break time="1.0s" />

A hundred-dollar bill isn't worth a hundred dollars because of the paper. It's worth a hundred dollars because people all over the world agree to hold it.
And while they hold it, someone else collects the interest.
