Why Google Lost $500 Billion in One Day

<break time="1.0s" />

October 29, 2026. Shares of Alphabet, the company behind Google and YouTube, fall twelve point six percent in a single day.
The worst day since the company went public, more than twenty years ago.

<break time="1.0s" />

Around five hundred billion dollars of market value, gone in hours. One of the biggest one-day losses for any company in stock market history.

<break time="1.0s" />

And here's the strange part. The night before, Google had reported that its revenue grew twenty-five percent. People were searching more than ever.
So what were investors so afraid of? To find out, we have to follow the money Google makes, and the money it's spending.
This is Billions and Bulls. Let's follow the money.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 1 – The Money Machine]
For twenty-five years, Google has run one of the most profitable businesses ever built. And it works like this.

<break time="1.0s" />

You type a question. Google shows you answers. And next to some of those answers, there are ads.
Advertisers pay when people click. Not when they see the ad. When they click.

<break time="1.0s" />

In the third quarter of 2026, Google's ads brought in eighty-two billion dollars. Out of total revenue of about one hundred twenty-eight billion.
Search alone brought in about sixty-three billion dollars. In three months.
A year earlier, search had brought in about fifty-six and a half billion. So it was still growing, about twelve percent a year. YouTube ads added another eleven billion.

<break time="1.0s" />

To keep that machine running, Google pays other companies to send it searches. Phone makers, browsers, websites. It calls these traffic acquisition costs.
Last quarter, they came to about sixteen billion dollars.
Court documents in a US antitrust case showed that in 2022, Google paid Apple alone about twenty billion dollars a year, to be the default search engine on its devices.

<break time="1.0s" />

Think about that. Google could pay twenty billion dollars a year to one partner, and still make enormous profits. Because each search was so valuable.
For decades, that was the deal. More searches meant more clicks. More clicks meant more money.

<break time="1.0s" />

But in October 2026, that link started to look broken.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 2 – The Number That Missed]
On paper, the quarter looked great. Revenue up twenty-five percent. Google said the number of searches was growing at its fastest pace in five years.

<break time="1.0s" />

But Wall Street looked at one line first. Search revenue. And it came in at sixty-three point three five billion dollars. Analysts had expected about sixty-four point eight five.
Even worse, it barely moved from the quarter before. Up about one tenth of one percent.

<break time="1.0s" />

Why does missing an estimate by one and a half billion dollars matter so much, for a company this big? Because a share price isn't a reward for what a company earned. It's a bet on what it will earn.
The estimates are what investors had already paid for. When the real number comes in lower, the price adjusts. And when the number that misses is the one that has carried the company for twenty-five years, it adjusts a lot.

<break time="1.0s" />

So, more searches. But almost no growth in search money. How?
Part of the answer came from Google itself.

<break time="1.0s" />

On the call with investors, a senior Google executive said that one reason search ad revenue was underperforming was Google's own AI Mode. The feature that answers your question directly, with artificial intelligence. It was showing fewer ads.
He said Google was working on it, and that ad experiments in AI Mode had shown promising results.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here's the problem in plain terms. The old Google gave you ten links, and ads next to them. You often had to click around to find what you wanted. Every click was a chance to earn money.
The new Google gives you the answer right away. That's better for you. But if you don't need to click, there's less to sell.

<break time="1.0s" />

Analysts at JPMorgan wrote that the slowdown in search was steeper than they expected.
In other words, Google is now competing with its own best product. And the old product is the one that pays the bills.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 3 – The Two Hundred Billion Dollar Shopping List]
If the money coming in was the first worry, the money going out was the second.

<break time="1.0s" />

Artificial intelligence needs data centers. Huge buildings full of chips, cooling systems and power lines. And they are incredibly expensive.
In the third quarter alone, Alphabet spent about fifty-one billion dollars on property and equipment. Mostly for AI.

<break time="1.0s" />

For the full year 2026, it expects to spend between one hundred ninety-five and two hundred five billion dollars. More than double what it spent in 2025. And it said spending would rise significantly again in 2027.
To put that in perspective, two hundred billion dollars is more than Google's entire revenue in 2019. Every dollar the company took in that year, and more, now goes into buildings and chips in a single year.
On top of that, it disclosed more than one trillion dollars in multi-year commitments for the infrastructure, energy and leases needed to run its AI systems.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now look at what that does to the cash. There's a measure called free cash flow. It's the cash a business brings in from its operations, minus what it spends on buildings and equipment. It's roughly the money left over.
A year earlier, Alphabet's free cash flow for the quarter was about twenty-four and a half billion dollars.
This time, it was negative. Minus nine billion dollars. The second negative quarter in a row.

<break time="1.0s" />

One of the most profitable companies in history was spending more cash than it was bringing in.

<break time="1.0s" />

If you're enjoying the story so far, subscribing helps the channel a lot. Because next, we follow where Google is getting the money to pay for all this.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 4 – Borrowing to Build]
When a company spends more cash than it makes, the money has to come from somewhere.

<break time="1.0s" />

In the third quarter, Alphabet sold bonds worth about twenty-eight point seven billion dollars. In other words, it borrowed.
It also did something that would have seemed unthinkable a few years ago. It sold new shares of itself. About twelve billion dollars' worth in the quarter, through a program that lets it sell shares gradually on the market.

<break time="1.0s" />

For years, Google did the opposite. It used its spare cash to buy back its own shares. In 2025 alone, it spent about forty-five billion dollars doing that.
Buying back shares means each remaining share owns a slightly bigger piece of the company. Selling new shares does the reverse. Each share owns a slightly smaller piece.

<break time="1.0s" />

So in about a year, Google went from handing cash back to shareholders, to asking investors and lenders for more.
None of this is illegal or unusual. Companies borrow to build all the time. But borrowing isn't free. Bonds have to be paid back, with interest. And in October 2026, interest rates were high. Earlier that month, the yield on the ten-year US Treasury had climbed above five percent, the highest level since 2002.

<break time="1.0s" />

So the question investors ask has changed. It's no longer, how much cash will Google return? It's, when will all this spending pay off?

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 5 – Who Fell With It]
When Alphabet fell, it didn't fall alone.

<break time="1.0s" />

Companies that sell equipment for data centers dropped too. The construction machinery maker Caterpillar fell about nine percent in early trading. Chip stocks slid.
Because if the biggest buyer starts to look nervous about spending, everyone who sells to it gets nervous too.

<break time="1.0s" />

Only one company had ever lost more value in a single day. Nvidia, in January 2025, when a cheap AI model from a Chinese startup made investors question how many expensive chips the world would really need. That day, Nvidia lost about five hundred eighty-nine billion dollars.
The two biggest one-day losses in history now came from the same question. Is all this AI spending going to pay off?

<break time="1.0s" />

And here's a detail that shows how big Alphabet is. That day, four out of every five stocks in the S&P 500 went up. The index still closed lower. Mostly because of one company.
If you own an index fund, you owned a piece of that drop.

<break time="1.0s" />

But there's another side, and it's important.
Even after the fall, Alphabet was still worth more than three trillion dollars. Its cloud business, which rents computing power to other companies, more than doubled its revenue, to thirty point seven billion dollars in the quarter. Its Gemini AI app reached one billion users.
So the AI money is real. Some of it is already coming in, just through a different door than search ads.

<break time="1.0s" />

And it was also part of a bigger pattern. The same day, The New York Times published its first report about Nvidia's chief executive, the story we told in a recent episode. Investors were already worried about how much the whole industry was spending on AI.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 6 – So, Why Did Google Lose $500 Billion?]
So let's put it together.

<break time="1.0s" />

Google didn't lose five hundred billion dollars because it had a bad quarter. Revenue grew twenty-five percent. It still made about twenty-six billion dollars in profit in three months.
It lost that value because investors changed their guess about the future.

<break time="1.0s" />

On one side, the old money machine, search ads, stopped growing, partly because Google's own AI answers questions without the need to click.
On the other side, the new machine costs about two hundred billion dollars a year to build, and Google is borrowing and selling shares to pay for it.

<break time="1.0s" />

Maybe it works. The cloud numbers suggest a lot of it might. Maybe in a few years, AI earns more than search ever did.
But for one day in October, investors decided they wanted to see more proof first. And they took half a trillion dollars off the table while they waited.

<break time="1.0s" />

For twenty-five years, Google got paid every time you had to keep looking.
Its new AI is designed so that you don't have to.
