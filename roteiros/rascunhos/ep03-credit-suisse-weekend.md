How a 167-Year-Old Bank Died in a Weekend

Friday, March 17, 2023. Credit Suisse is one of the biggest banks in the world. It is one hundred and sixty-seven years old. It helped build Switzerland.

<break time="1.0s" />

Sunday night, it's gone. Sold to its oldest rival for about three billion Swiss francs. And sixteen billion francs of its bonds are wiped out. Erased.

<break time="1.0s" />

A bank that old doesn't die in one weekend. Not really. So we're going to follow the money out the door. Where it went, who paid, and who walked away richer.

[Part 1 – The Railway Bank]
The story starts in 1856, in Zurich.
Switzerland wanted railways. And a politician and businessman named Alfred Escher wanted them built with Swiss money, not French or German money.
So he started a bank to pay for them. It was called the Schweizerische Kreditanstalt. Later, the world would know it as Credit Suisse.

<break time="1.0s" />

The idea was popular. Within days, investors asked for shares worth many times what the bank was offering.
In its early years, about a quarter of its revenue came from a single railway company. One that Escher himself ran.
That's how close the bank was to power from day one. The man who ran the railway also ran the bank that lent to it. Later, he led the company that built the Gotthard line, the railway through the Alps.

<break time="1.0s" />

From there, the bank grew with the country. It moved into a grand building on Paradeplatz, the square at the heart of Swiss banking. It opened an office in New York in 1870.
And over the decades, it bought its way into Wall Street, including the American investment bank First Boston.

<break time="1.0s" />

By the 2000s, Credit Suisse was two banks in one.
A Swiss bank. Safe, quiet, managing the money of rich families from all over the world.
And an investment bank. Bigger risks. Bigger bonuses. Bigger swings.

<break time="1.0s" />

When the financial crisis hit in 2008, its Swiss rival, UBS, needed a rescue from the Swiss state.
Credit Suisse didn't. It raised money from private investors instead, and came out looking like the stronger of the two.

<break time="1.0s" />

Remember that. Because fifteen years later, the roles would flip completely.

<break time="1.0s" />

So what went wrong? It wasn't one thing. It was a long list. And it starts with a loan to a fishing company.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 2 – A Decade of Scandals]
Between 2013 and 2016, Credit Suisse helped arrange about one billion dollars in loans and bonds for state-linked companies in Mozambique. On paper, a lot of it was for a tuna fishing fleet.
Much of the money was diverted, through kickbacks to Credit Suisse bankers and Mozambican officials.
In 2021, the bank agreed to pay about four hundred and seventy-five million dollars to American and British authorities.

<break time="1.0s" />

In 2019, it got stranger. Credit Suisse was caught hiring private detectives to follow a former top executive, who had left to join UBS.
The Swiss regulator later found that the bank had planned seven spying operations between 2016 and 2019. The chief executive, Tidjane Thiam, resigned in early 2020.

<break time="1.0s" />

Then came 2021. The year it all got expensive.
In March, Credit Suisse froze about ten billion dollars in investment funds linked to Greensill Capital, a lending firm that collapsed days later. More than a thousand investors couldn't get their money out.

<break time="1.0s" />

Just weeks after that, a private investment firm called Archegos imploded. Credit Suisse had lent it huge amounts of money. When Archegos couldn't pay, Credit Suisse booked a charge of four point four billion Swiss francs, and warned of more losses to come.
Other banks lost money on Archegos too. None lost as much as Credit Suisse.
And that's a story we'll follow in its own episode.

<break time="1.0s" />

In January 2022, the bank's new chairman resigned, after an internal probe reportedly found he had broken COVID quarantine rules.
And in June 2022, a Swiss court convicted Credit Suisse of failing to stop a Bulgarian cocaine gang from laundering money through its accounts. It was reportedly the first criminal trial of a major Swiss bank in the country.

<break time="1.0s" />

None of these alone could kill a bank. But each one sent the same message to clients.
Something here isn't working.

<break time="1.0s" />

And in banking, that message is the most expensive one there is.

[Part 3 – The Slow-Motion Run]
Here's the thing about a bank. It doesn't keep your money in a vault.
It lends it out. It invests it. At any moment, only a small part is available as cash.
That works perfectly. As long as everyone doesn't ask for their money back at the same time.

<break time="1.0s" />

In October 2022, Credit Suisse announced a radical rescue plan. Cut about nine thousand jobs. Split off the investment bank. Raise around four billion francs in new capital.
Its new anchor investor was the Saudi National Bank, which agreed to put in up to one and a half billion dollars, for a stake of just under ten percent.

<break time="1.0s" />

But clients were already leaving.
In the last three months of 2022, they pulled out one hundred and ten billion Swiss francs. That's more than a billion francs walking out the door, every single day, for three months.
For the full year, the bank lost seven point three billion francs.

<break time="1.0s" />

Then came March 2023.
On March 10, a bank in California, Silicon Valley Bank, collapsed. Its customers had tried to pull out about forty-two billion dollars in a single day. With an app, a run no longer takes weeks. It takes hours.
Suddenly, every investor in the world was looking for the next weak bank.
On March 14, Credit Suisse published its delayed annual report. It admitted "material weaknesses" in how it checked its own financial reporting.

<break time="1.0s" />

The next morning, the chairman of the Saudi National Bank was asked on television if his bank would put in more money.
His answer: "Absolutely not." He explained that going above ten percent would trigger new rules from regulators.
It was a technical reason. But markets didn't hear the reason. They heard the word "not."
Credit Suisse shares fell as much as twenty-five percent that day, to a record low.

<break time="1.0s" />

That night, the Swiss National Bank said it would lend Credit Suisse up to fifty billion francs. The next morning, Credit Suisse said it would take the money.
The shares bounced, for about a day. It wasn't enough. In the first three months of 2023, clients pulled another sixty-one billion francs. And the bank said the heaviest losses came in the second half of March.

<break time="1.0s" />

By Friday, the people in charge knew the bank would not survive another week.

<break time="1.5s" />
```

[Part 4 – The Weekend]
Why a weekend? Because banks are safest when markets are closed.
The Swiss had until Monday morning, when trading would open in Asia, to find an answer. Otherwise, the run could turn into a stampede.

<break time="1.0s" />

So, over the weekend, the Swiss government, the central bank, the financial regulator and UBS sat down to make a deal.
At Friday's close, Credit Suisse had been worth around eight billion dollars on the stock market, according to news reports.

<break time="1.0s" />

On Sunday evening, March 19, they announced it.
UBS would take over Credit Suisse. The price: three billion Swiss francs, paid in UBS shares. That's about seventy-six centimes for each Credit Suisse share. Less than one franc, for a share that had once traded for far more.

<break time="1.0s" />

To make it work, the government used emergency powers. Shareholders of both banks didn't get a vote.
The Swiss state also offered UBS a safety net. If the riskiest parts of Credit Suisse lost money, UBS would take the first five billion francs of losses. The government would cover the next nine billion.
And the Swiss National Bank promised up to one hundred billion francs in extra loans, backed by the government, so the combined bank would never run out of cash.

<break time="1.0s" />

There were other options on the table. The government reportedly considered taking over Credit Suisse itself, at least for a while. But that would have put the whole bank, and all its risks, on the taxpayers' shoulders. A sale to UBS looked like the smaller risk.

<break time="1.0s" />

Remember 2008? UBS was the bank that needed a rescue.
Now, fifteen years later, UBS was the rescuer. And Credit Suisse, the bank that had stood on its own, was the one being saved. Or rather, swallowed.

<break time="1.0s" />

But the biggest shock of the weekend wasn't the price.
It was what happened to a group of investors most people had never heard of.

<break time="1.0s" />

[Part 5 – The Bonds That Went to Zero]
After the 2008 crisis, regulators wanted banks to have a cushion. Something that would absorb losses before taxpayers had to step in.
One answer was a special kind of bond, called AT1. Additional Tier 1.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here's how it works. Investors lend the bank money, and get a higher interest rate than on normal bonds.
In exchange, they accept a risk. If the bank gets into deep trouble, the bonds can be converted into shares, or written off.
That's why people in the market also call them CoCos. Contingent convertibles. They're bonds that can stop being bonds.
Credit Suisse's AT1 bonds had a clause for exactly this moment. If the bank received extraordinary support from the government, they could be wiped out.

<break time="1.0s" />

That's exactly what happened. The Swiss regulator ordered Credit Suisse's AT1 bonds, about sixteen billion francs of them, written down to zero.
Not a discount. Zero.

<break time="1.0s" />

But here's why investors were furious.
Normally, shareholders are first in line to lose. They own the company, so they take the first hit. Bondholders lose only after the shareholders are wiped out.
At Credit Suisse, it was the other way around. Shareholders still got about three billion francs in UBS shares. The AT1 bondholders got nothing.

<break time="1.0s" />

The Swiss said the bonds' own terms allowed it, once the government stepped in.
The rest of the market panicked anyway. On Monday, AT1 bonds of other banks around the world fell sharply, as investors wondered if their own bonds could be next.
But the very next day, regulators in the European Union put out a statement. In Europe, they said, shareholders absorb losses first. AT1 comes after. The Bank of England said much the same.

<break time="1.0s" />

Around three thousand bondholders went to court in Switzerland, filing more than three hundred and sixty complaints.
And in October 2025, the Federal Administrative Court ruled that the write-down lacked a sufficient legal basis.
But that wasn't the end. The regulator and UBS appealed to Switzerland's Federal Supreme Court. As this video was made, the bonds were still worth zero, waiting for a final answer.

<break time="1.5s" />
```

[Part 6 – Who Paid, and Who Profited]
So let's add it up.

<break time="1.0s" />

Credit Suisse shareholders lost almost everything. Shares that had been worth billions became a few centimes each.
AT1 bondholders lost about sixteen billion francs. At least for now.
And thousands of employees lost their jobs. Since the takeover, UBS has reportedly cut about seventeen thousand five hundred positions.
The name went too. On May 31, 2024, Credit Suisse was legally merged into UBS. After one hundred and sixty-eight years, the bank founded to build Swiss railways simply stopped existing. On a Friday, quietly, with no weekend drama at all.

<break time="1.0s" />

What about Swiss taxpayers? This is where it gets surprising.
In August 2023, UBS ended the government's nine-billion-franc guarantee, and the central bank backstop. It said it didn't need them anymore.
The government says it didn't lose a franc. It actually collected around two hundred million francs in fees.

<break time="1.0s" />

And UBS? In the second quarter of 2023, it reported a profit of about twenty-nine billion dollars.
Almost all of it came from one accounting line, called negative goodwill. In plain English: UBS paid far less for Credit Suisse than its assets were worth on paper. The difference showed up as profit.

<break time="1.0s" />

But the story isn't over. It moved.
Switzerland now has one giant bank instead of two. If UBS ever got into trouble, there's no rival left to rescue it.
So lawmakers want UBS to hold much more capital. In September 2026, Switzerland's upper house backed a plan that UBS says could cost it about sixteen billion dollars in extra capital. UBS has pushed back hard, and has reportedly looked at options that include moving its headquarters.

<break time="1.0s" />

A Swiss parliamentary inquiry put the main blame on Credit Suisse's own managers, over many years. It also criticized the regulator, for giving the bank too much room on its capital back in 2017.

<break time="1.0s" />

So, how does a one hundred and sixty-seven-year-old bank die in a weekend?

<break time="1.0s" />

It doesn't. The weekend was only the funeral.
The money left over years. Scandal by scandal. Loss by loss. One client at a time.
A bank runs on money. But money runs on trust. And when the trust leaves, the money follows.
