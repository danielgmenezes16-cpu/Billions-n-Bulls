[Part 1 – From $400 Million to $1.5 Trillion]
First, what does it mean to go public?

<break time="1.0s" />

A private company is owned by its founders, its employees and the investors who funded it. Their shares can't easily be sold.
In an initial public offering, or IPO, the company sells new shares to the public for the first time. After that, anyone can buy and sell them on a stock exchange.

<break time="1.0s" />

Anthropic was founded in 2021 by a group of former OpenAI employees. It's also set up as a public benefit corporation, a legal structure that lets it weigh a stated public mission alongside profits.

<break time="1.0s" />

Its growth has been unusual by any standard. According to its filings, revenue went from about four hundred million dollars in 2024, to about four point six billion in 2025.
In the first nine months of 2026, it was thirty-three point two billion dollars. More than twelve times the same period a year earlier.

<break time="1.0s" />

The company also reported what's called a run rate of about eighty-one billion dollars. That's a projection. Roughly, it takes the most recent four weeks of usage and multiplies it out to a full year. It shows momentum, but it's not money already earned.

<break time="1.0s" />

In May 2026, private investors valued Anthropic at about nine hundred sixty-five billion dollars. A few months later, there was talk of a two trillion dollar IPO.
Then AI stocks sold off. Google had its worst day ever, and Nvidia's shares slid. Anthropic set a lower price range.
In the end, demand was strong enough to price above that range. About one point five trillion dollars.

[Part 2 – Where the $81 Billion Goes]
Now, the money. When people hear that an IPO raised eighty-one billion dollars, many assume the founders pocketed it. That's usually not how it works.

<break time="1.0s" />

There are two kinds of shares sold in an IPO. New shares, created by the company. The money from those goes into the company's own bank account.
And existing shares, sold by people who already owned them. That money goes to those sellers.

<break time="1.0s" />

In Anthropic's original plan, about eight hundred one million of the eight hundred twenty-five million shares on offer were new shares from the company. So the vast majority of the money goes to Anthropic itself, not to individuals.
The offering was later made bigger, but the basic split is the same idea.

<break time="1.0s" />

And Anthropic needs it. Building and running AI models takes enormous amounts of computing power.
According to its filings, the company has more than nine hundred billion dollars in purchase commitments and leases for computing and infrastructure. Some of them running for almost two decades.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now here's an interesting detail. Alongside the IPO, Anthropic lined up another twenty-six billion dollars in a private sale of shares to some of its biggest suppliers. Companies including Nvidia, Google and Microsoft.
In other words, companies that sell Anthropic chips and computing power are also buying a piece of it. Some of that money is likely to come right back to them, as payment for computing.
That kind of circle is becoming common in AI. And it's a story we'll follow in our next episode.

[Part 3 – The Early Backers]
So who are the big winners on paper? Start with the early investors.

<break time="1.0s" />

Two names stand out. Amazon and Google. Both invested in Anthropic years ago, when it was a much smaller company.
After the IPO and the private sale, an updated filing reportedly showed Amazon holding about fourteen point seven percent of the company. Google, about thirteen point seven percent.

<break time="1.0s" />

Some simple math. At a value of one and a half trillion dollars, a stake of fourteen point seven percent is worth about two hundred twenty billion dollars. Thirteen point seven percent is worth about two hundred five billion.
That's on paper, and it moves every day with the share price. But it's one of the best investments in the history of either company.

<break time="1.0s" />

And the relationship goes deeper than ownership.
Amazon and Google aren't just shareholders. They also sell Anthropic's models through their cloud platforms. In 2025, each of them reportedly accounted for about twelve percent of Anthropic's revenue. And in the first half of 2026, nearly half of Anthropic's revenue came through cloud marketplaces.
Anthropic has also reportedly committed to spend more than one hundred billion dollars on Amazon's cloud over a decade.

<break time="1.0s" />

So for Amazon and Google, Anthropic is an investment, a customer, and a product they sell. All at the same time.

[Part 4 – The People Inside]
Like most tech companies, Anthropic pays its employees partly in stock. And going public turns that stock into something they can eventually sell.

<break time="1.0s" />

The filing shows how big that is. About two point nine billion dollars of stock compensation is recognized at the IPO. And around twenty-three point seven billion dollars more over the following two years or so.
Employees usually can't sell right away. IPOs typically come with a lock-up period, often several months, so that insiders don't all rush for the exit on day one.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now for the strange part. For the first half of 2026, Anthropic reported a net loss of about eighty billion dollars.
That sounds catastrophic. But most of it, more than seventy-two billion dollars, came from an accounting rule. Anthropic had sold investors notes that could turn into shares. As the company's value went up, those notes became more valuable, and the company had to record that increase as a loss.
In other words, the better the company did, the bigger the loss on paper.

<break time="1.0s" />

The operating numbers tell a different story. In the second quarter of 2026, Anthropic made an operating profit of about nine hundred seventy-four million dollars. It was the only profitable quarter in the filing, but it was a first.
