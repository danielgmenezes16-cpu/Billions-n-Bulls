[Part 3 – The Price of One Person]
So let's look at the money. CNN put the drop since Huang admitted the affair and the investigation began at about seventeen percent. Seven hundred fifty-one billion dollars of market value.

<break time="1.0s" />

To understand why a company can lose that much over one person, you need to know what a share price really is.
A share isn't paying for what a company owns today. It's a bet on everything the company will earn in the future. Years and years of future profits, squeezed into one number.

<break time="1.0s" />

So when a founder leaves, investors start asking new questions. Who makes the big bets now? Who closes the next deal with a government, or a tech giant? Will the best engineers stay?
Every unanswered question makes that future a little less certain. And a less certain future is worth less today.

<break time="1.0s" />

There's a name for this. Key person risk. Companies even warn about it in their annual reports. It's the risk that the business depends so much on one or a few people, that losing them would hurt.

<break time="1.0s" />

It has happened before, at a much smaller scale. In 2019, McDonald's fired its chief executive, Steve Easterbrook, for a consensual relationship with an employee, which broke company policy.
He left with a severance package estimated at around forty million dollars. Then the company sued, saying he had lied about other relationships. In 2021, he agreed to return more than one hundred five million dollars, and apologized.

<break time="1.0s" />

The lesson boards took from cases like that is simple. Rules about relationships at work apply at the very top too. Maybe especially at the top, where one person's choices can move billions.

<break time="1.0s" />

But history also has the opposite lesson. When Steve Jobs stepped down at Apple in 2011, many people wondered if the company could survive without its founder.
Under his successor, Tim Cook, Apple went on to become one of the most valuable companies in the world. A founder leaving is a risk. It isn't a sentence.

<break time="1.0s" />

If you're enjoying the story so far, subscribing helps the channel a lot. Because next, we'll find out who actually lost those seven hundred fifty billion dollars.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 4 – Who Lost the $751 Billion]
When a stock falls, the money doesn't go to anyone. There's no pile of cash that moves from one place to another. The value just isn't there anymore, at least on paper.
But somebody's account balance goes down. So whose?

<break time="1.0s" />

Start with Huang himself. Some simple math. Three point six percent of seven hundred fifty-one billion dollars is about twenty-seven billion. On paper, the person who lost the most from his own exit was probably him.

<break time="1.0s" />

Then there are Nvidia's employees, many of whom are paid partly in company stock. For years, that stock made a lot of them rich. In November, it went the other way.

<break time="1.0s" />

And then, there's almost everyone else. Nvidia is one of the biggest companies in the S&P 500. In mid-November, according to one analysis, it made up about six percent of an S&P 500 index fund.
So if you have a retirement account with an index fund, there's a good chance you owned a small piece of that drop. Without ever buying a single Nvidia share on purpose.

<break time="1.0s" />

But here's the twist. Not all of that fall was about Huang.
Nvidia's shares had already started sliding before the scandal broke. Since the record in early October, CNN reports, the stock was down about thirty-five percent. Roughly two trillion dollars.
Part of that was a wider fear about the artificial intelligence boom. On October 29, the same day the Times story came out, Google's parent company had the worst day in its history on the stock market.

<break time="1.0s" />

So the honest answer is that nobody can split the drop neatly into Huang's part and the market's part. Investors were nervous already. The scandal gave them one more reason.

[Part 6 – So, What Is One CEO Worth?]
So let's put it together.

<break time="1.0s" />
