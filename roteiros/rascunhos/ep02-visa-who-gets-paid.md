```
[Part 1 – The Two-Second Trip]
Say you spend one hundred dollars at a store, and you pay with a rewards credit card.
You don't even swipe anymore. In September 2025, Visa says, four out of five of its in-person payments around the world were made with a tap.

<break time="1.0s" />

The moment you tap, the card reader sends a message to the store's bank, or to the payment company the store uses. People in the industry call it the acquirer.
The acquirer passes the message to the card network. In this case, Visa.
And Visa sends it to the bank that gave you the card. The issuer.

<break time="1.0s" />

Your bank checks two things. Is this really you? And do you have the money, or the credit, to pay?
If the answer is yes, the approval races all the way back. Beep.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now multiply that by the whole planet. In its 2025 fiscal year, Visa says its own network processed two hundred and fifty-eight billion transactions. That's roughly eight thousand every single second, day and night.

<break time="1.0s" />

But the beep is only a promise. No money has moved yet.
That happens later, usually overnight, in two steps the industry calls clearing and settlement. The store's bank sends in the day's purchases. The banks settle up through Visa. And only then does money land in the store's account.

<break time="1.0s" />

And here's the thing. It isn't your one hundred dollars.
Your bank sends the money, minus a fee. Visa charges its own fees along the way. And the store's payment company takes a cut too.
By the time it arrives, the store keeps somewhere around ninety-seven dollars. With some cards, a little more. With others, a little less.

<break time="1.0s" />

Three dollars doesn't sound like much. But it adds up.
In 2025, American merchants paid a record of more than one hundred and ninety-eight billion dollars in card processing fees, according to the Nilson Report.
That's more than half a billion dollars. Every single day.

<break time="1.0s" />

So who ends up with all that money? Let's start with the name on the card.
```

```
[Part 2 – The Toll Road]
The story starts in 1958, in Fresno, California.
Bank of America mailed about sixty thousand credit cards to people who had never asked for one. No application. The card just showed up in the mailbox, ready to use.
It was called the BankAmericard. And stores that took it reportedly paid a fee of around six percent.

<break time="1.0s" />

The launch was messy. Fraud and unpaid bills piled up. But the idea spread. Other banks joined in, and in 1976 the system got a new name. Visa.
For decades, Visa was owned by the banks that used it. Then, in 2008, it went public. The IPO reportedly raised about seventeen point nine billion dollars, the biggest in American history at the time.

<break time="1.0s" />

And here's what most people get wrong about Visa.
Visa doesn't give you a credit card. It doesn't lend you money. It doesn't set your interest rate. And it doesn't lose a cent if you never pay your bill.
Visa says so itself, in its annual report. It is not a financial institution. It does not issue cards, and it does not extend credit.

<break time="1.0s" />

So what is it? Think of it as a toll road for money.
Banks own the cars. Visa owns the road. And every time money drives across it, Visa charges a small toll.

<break time="1.0s" />

The tolls are tiny. But the traffic is enormous.
In the twelve months to September 2025, about fourteen point two trillion dollars in payments moved on Visa's network, on nearly five billion Visa cards and other payment credentials.
Visa charges banks for the volume they push across the road. It charges for every message it processes. And some of its richest tolls come from cross-border payments, when you pay in another currency. That line alone brought in more than fourteen billion dollars.

<break time="1.0s" />

But there's a twist. Visa also pays the banks. Almost sixteen billion dollars last year, in what it calls client incentives. Money to put Visa's name on more cards, and to keep the traffic coming.

<break time="1.0s" />

Add it all up, and Visa brought in forty billion dollars in net revenue in its 2025 fiscal year. Its net income was about twenty billion. Visa kept roughly half of everything it made as profit.
Spread across all those payments, that's roughly twenty-eight cents for every hundred dollars on its network.

<break time="1.0s" />

So if the store loses about three dollars, and Visa takes only a fraction of that... where does the rest go?
To the biggest player in this story. And it's not the one whose name is on your card.
```

```
[Part 3 – The Fee Visa Sets, but Doesn't Keep]
It's called the interchange fee.
When you pay with a card, the store's bank pays a fee to your bank. Visa sets the default rates, and publishes them in a public table that runs for dozens of pages.
But Visa doesn't keep that fee. Your bank does.

<break time="1.0s" />

Why does it exist at all? Visa's argument is that it balances the system. Your bank takes the risk that you won't pay. It fights fraud. It gives you time to pay. And the store gets a customer who can spend money they don't have in their pocket.

<break time="1.0s" />

But the rate depends on the card you carry.
According to Visa's own rate table, for a purchase at a regular store, a basic credit card carries a fee of one point four three percent, plus ten cents. On our hundred dollars, that's a dollar fifty-three.
A premium card like Visa Infinite can carry two point three percent, plus ten cents. Two dollars and forty cents. To your bank.
Buy the same thing online, and the premium rate can go up to two point six percent, plus ten cents. Two dollars and seventy cents.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now pay with a debit card from a big bank instead. In the United States, that fee is capped by law at twenty-one cents, plus five hundredths of a percent, plus one cent for fraud prevention. On the same hundred dollars, that's about twenty-seven cents.

<break time="1.0s" />

Same store. Same hundred dollars. About nine times more money for the bank, just because of the card in your wallet.

<break time="1.0s" />

So why would a bank want you to carry the expensive card?
Because that fee is what pays for the perks. The points. The cash back. The travel miles. The airport lounges.
Your bank collects more from the store, and hands some of it back to you as rewards. The more you spend, the more it collects. So banks compete hard for the people who spend the most.

<break time="1.0s" />

That's why the cards with the best rewards are usually the ones that cost stores the most.
And the store can't see that coming. The card is in your pocket until the very last second.

<break time="1.0s" />

Quick thing. If you like videos that follow the money, subscribe. It costs nothing. And unlike your rewards points... nobody else is paying for it.
```

```
[Part 4 – The People Who Pay Without Swiping]
So the store pays the fee. But who pays the store?
Stores have a few options. In most U.S. states, they're allowed to add a surcharge for credit cards. Under Visa's rules, it's capped at three percent. Some gas stations do it, and give you a cash price.
But most stores don't. Surcharges annoy customers. So instead, they raise their prices a little... for everyone.

<break time="1.0s" />

And everyone includes people paying with cash. And people paying with a basic debit card. They pay the same higher price, and they get no points back.

<break time="1.0s" />

In 2010, economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston tried to measure how big that hidden transfer is.
They estimated that, on average, each household paying with cash was paying one hundred and forty-nine dollars a year to card users. And each card-paying household was receiving one thousand, one hundred and thirty-three dollars a year from cash users.

<break time="1.0s" />

They also estimated that about seventy-nine percent of banks' revenue from credit card merchant fees came, indirectly, from cash payers.
And the money flowed from poorer households to richer ones. The lowest-income households paid about twenty-one dollars a year. The highest-income households received about seven hundred and fifty.

<break time="1.0s" />

That study is from 2010, and some payments experts dispute its assumptions. But newer research points the same way.
A 2023 study published by the Federal Reserve estimated that rewards cards redistribute about fifteen billion dollars a year. From less educated people to more educated ones. And from poorer areas to richer ones.

<break time="1.0s" />

There's a second split, too. Inside the group of people who do use credit cards.
The people who gain the most are the ones who pay their bill in full every month. They collect the points, and never pay a cent of interest.
The ones who carry a balance end up paying more in interest than they ever get back in rewards.

<break time="1.0s" />

To be fair, cards aren't just a cost. They protect you from fraud. They let stores skip the risk of handling piles of cash. And the industry argues that cards help stores sell more, which pays for part of the fee.

<break time="1.0s" />

But the points? Those were never free.

<break time="1.5s" />
```

```
[Part 5 – The Twenty-Year Fight]
Stores have been fighting these fees for a long time.
In 2005, merchants filed a giant class action against Visa, Mastercard and the big banks. More than twenty years later, it still isn't fully over.

<break time="1.0s" />

In 2012, the two sides agreed on a settlement worth about seven billion dollars. An appeals court threw it out in 2016. A second deal, for at least five and a half billion dollars, was approved in 2019. That covered the money. The fight over the rules kept going.

<break time="1.0s" />

Meanwhile, in 2010, Congress stepped in, but only for debit cards. The Durbin Amendment led to the cap we saw earlier, twenty-one cents plus a little, for big banks. Credit cards were left alone.

<break time="1.0s" />

And here's where the money found a side door. The cap only applies to banks with more than ten billion dollars in assets. Smaller banks can still charge the old, higher rates.
So a whole new kind of company grew up around that gap. App-based banks that partner with small banks. Chime, for example, said in its 2025 IPO filing that debit card interchange made up about half of its revenue.

<break time="1.0s" />

In 2024, the U.S. Justice Department sued Visa, accusing it of illegally monopolizing debit payments. The government said more than sixty percent of U.S. debit transactions run on Visa's network, and that Visa collects more than seven billion dollars a year in fees from them.
Visa denies it, and is fighting the case. But in 2025, a judge refused to throw it out.

<break time="1.0s" />

Back in the big merchant case, a judge rejected a new settlement in 2024, saying it didn't go far enough.
So they tried again. In June 2026, a federal judge gave preliminary approval to another deal.
It would cut credit card interchange rates by a tenth of a percentage point, for five years. It would cap the rate on standard credit cards at one point two five percent, for eight years. And it would let stores refuse some premium cards.

<break time="1.0s" />

A tenth of a percentage point. On our hundred dollars, that's ten cents. The premium card goes from two dollars forty to two dollars thirty.
Some of the biggest retailers objected that it's far too little. As this video was made, the deal still needed final approval.

<break time="1.5s" />
```

```
[Part 6 – So, Who Pays for Your Points?]
For a very different answer, look across the Atlantic.
In 2015, the European Union capped interchange fees. Point three percent on consumer credit cards. Point two percent on debit cards.
On a hundred-euro purchase, that's thirty cents for the bank. Not two dollars and forty.

<break time="1.0s" />

A study commissioned by the European Commission found that interchange on consumer cards dropped by about thirty-five percent. More than two billion euros a year that stayed with stores.
And with fees capped that low, banks have far less money to hand back as points. In the UK, some banks reportedly dropped their rewards cards before the cap even took effect.

<break time="1.0s" />

So Europe made a trade. Cheaper fees for stores. Fewer perks for card users. America made the opposite one.

<break time="1.0s" />

So, when you tap your card, who really gets paid?

<break time="1.0s" />

Visa takes a toll. A few cents, on trillions of dollars. And it pays some of that back to the banks, to keep its name on the card.
The store's payment company takes its cut.
Your bank takes the biggest slice, and hands some of it back to you as points.

<break time="1.0s" />

And the bill is split between the store, its customers... and people who never tapped a card at all.

Your points aren't free. Everyone at the checkout chips in.
```
