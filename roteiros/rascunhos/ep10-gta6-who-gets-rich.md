```
[Part 2 – The Console's Cut]
Let's say you buy the game digitally, straight from the store on your PlayStation or Xbox.

<break time="1.0s" />

The first slice goes to the company that made your console. Sony, or Microsoft.
For years, the standard cut on console stores has been about thirty percent. On an eighty-dollar game, that's about twenty-four dollars. Before the publisher sees anything.

<break time="1.0s" />

Why so much? Because consoles work a bit like printers. The machine is often sold close to cost, sometimes at a loss, to get it into as many homes as possible.
The money is made later, on the games and subscriptions people buy for it. Every game sold on the console's store pays a toll.
The cut also pays for the store itself. The servers that deliver a huge game to millions of homes on the same day, the payment processing, refunds and fraud checks.

<break time="1.0s" />

Microsoft has looked at lowering that toll before. Internal documents showed it once considered cutting its console share to twelve percent. A spokesperson later said that change wouldn't happen on Xbox.
And since Grand Theft Auto Six is only on consoles at launch, with no PC version yet, every single digital copy goes through one of those two stores.

<break time="1.0s" />

So for each digital copy, about twenty-four dollars goes to Sony or Microsoft, and about fifty-six dollars goes to Take-Two.
If tens of millions of people buy digitally, the console makers earn billions from a game they didn't make.

<break time="1.5s" />
```

```
[Part 3 – The Box on the Shelf]
Now let's say you buy a physical copy instead, at a store.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here, the split is harder to see, because nobody publishes it. But one consultant who advises investors in the games industry, Serkan Toto, has laid out a typical example.

The store keeps around thirty percent. It has rent, staff and shelves to pay for.
Then the console maker still gets a cut, even though the game never touched its online store. Around fifteen percent, as a licensing fee for making a game for its machine.

Using those rough percentages on an eighty-dollar game, the publisher keeps around forty dollars from a physical copy. Compared with about fifty-six from a digital one.
That gap helps explain why game companies love digital sales. And why physical game shelves keep getting smaller.

And on top of the eighty dollars, there's one more slice most people forget. Sales tax, or value-added tax in Europe. That one goes to the government, before anyone in the games business sees a cent.
```

```
[Part 4 – Inside Take-Two]
So Take-Two ends up with somewhere between forty and fifty-six dollars per copy. Is that profit? Not even close.

<break time="1.0s" />

First, that money has to pay for the game itself. Rockstar Games, the studio inside Take-Two that makes Grand Theft Auto, has spent years building it, with studios in several countries.
Take-Two has never said how much Grand Theft Auto Six cost. For comparison, Grand Theft Auto Five reportedly cost around one hundred thirty-seven million dollars to develop. The new one is widely expected to have cost far more.

<break time="1.0s" />

Then there's marketing, servers, customer support, and the people who will keep updating the game for years.

<break time="1.0s" />

And here's a detail that shows how much rides on this one game. In the quarter before launch, Take-Two actually lost money. Fifty-four million dollars. Its basketball game sold less than expected.
Its shares still rose more than eight percent the next morning, because investors were betting on one thing. Grand Theft Auto Six.

<break time="1.0s" />

So in a sense, the eighty dollars you pay isn't just buying a game. It's paying back years of spending that happened before you ever saw a trailer.

<break time="1.0s" />

And it's worth knowing where Take-Two's money usually comes from. In its last full fiscal year, about seventy-eight percent of its sales came from what it calls recurrent consumer spending. Money players spend after they've bought a game: in-game currency, extra content, subscriptions.
In other words, most of the time, the first purchase isn't where most of the money is. Remember that number. We'll come back to it.

<break time="1.5s" />
```

```
[Part 5 – The British Taxpayer's Share]
Now for the part that surprises most people.

<break time="1.0s" />

The Grand Theft Auto games are set in fictional versions of American cities. But the series' lead studio, Rockstar North, is in Edinburgh, Scotland.
And the United Kingdom has a tax relief for video games. If a game passes a test showing it's culturally British, the studio can claim back part of what it spends making it.

<break time="1.0s" />

Grand Theft Auto Five passed that test in 2015.
The test isn't only about where a game is set. Points can also come from where the work is done, and who does it. A game about a fictional Los Angeles, made largely in Edinburgh, can qualify.
In 2024, Britain replaced the old relief with a new credit. Studios can now claim back thirty-four percent of qualifying spending, before tax.
According to an investigation by the Scottish newspaper The Scotsman, Rockstar claimed about three hundred sixty million pounds through that relief between 2015 and 2023. About thirty-five percent of all the money paid out under the scheme. A later report said the company received more than seventy million pounds in a single year, 2024 to 2025.

<break time="1.0s" />

A research group called TaxWatch UK went further. In reports from 2019 and 2020, it said that Rockstar's UK companies had paid no UK corporation tax over the previous ten years, even though their American parent company was making large profits. And it said the big claims were likely linked to the production of the next Grand Theft Auto.

<break time="1.0s" />

There's another side to this. The relief is legal, and it's designed to do exactly this: attract big game productions to Britain.
In response to the criticism, the company's side has said the program helped it create well over a thousand long-term, highly skilled jobs in the UK. And the industry's trade body says every pound invested through the relief brings back about four pounds in value to the economy.

<break time="1.0s" />

So when you buy Grand Theft Auto Six, part of the cost of making it was already covered. By British taxpayers.

<break time="1.5s" />
```
