[Part 1 – The Zero-Dollar Trade]
When you tap "Buy," your order usually doesn't go straight to the stock exchange.
It goes to a market maker, a firm like Citadel Securities, that pays your broker for the right to fill it.

<break time="1.0s" />

It's called payment for order flow.
The market maker earns a tiny slice of the spread: the gap between what buyers pay and what sellers receive.

<break time="1.0s" />

Fractions of a cent per share.
Millions of times a day.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here's what that looks like.
Imagine a stock where buyers are bidding 99 dollars and 98 cents, and sellers are asking 100 dollars and 2 cents.
That four-cent gap is the spread.
A market maker stands in the middle. It buys from the sellers, sells to the buyers, and keeps the difference.

<break time="1.0s" />

To win your order, it will often give you a slightly better price than the one on your screen. Maybe 100 dollars and 1 cent, instead of 100 dollars and 2 cents.
You save a penny. The market maker keeps the rest... minus a small cut it pays your broker for sending you there.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now multiply that by millions of orders.
In October 2025, Robinhood sent its customers' stock orders to six of these firms: Virtu, G1, Jane Street, Citadel Securities, Hudson River Trading, and Two Sigma.
Every single one of them paid for the privilege.
Together, about 40 million dollars. In one month.

<break time="1.0s" />

But stocks are the smaller part of this story.
The real money is somewhere most beginners never look... and we'll get there.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 2 – The Firm in the Middle]
First, meet the biggest of these firms.
Citadel Securities says it handles more than 35 percent of all retail trading volume in the United States, and about 30 percent of all US-listed options trading.

<break time="1.0s" />

In the first three months of 2025 alone, it reportedly made a record 3.4 billion dollars in trading revenue.

<break time="1.0s" />

So why would a firm that big pay for orders from people buying three shares at a time?

<break time="1.0s" />

Because your order is one of the safest orders in the market.
When a giant hedge fund starts buying, it might be the first slice of a huge position, and the price is about to move against whoever sold to it.
Your order is just... your order. Small, random, and unlikely to know something the market doesn't.

<break time="1.0s" />

Think of it like car insurance. Insurers love careful drivers, because they rarely cost them anything.
To a market maker, retail orders are the careful drivers of the stock market.
That's the best flow there is. So it pays for the right to trade against it.

<break time="1.0s" />

And that creates the conflict critics point to: your broker is being paid by the firm on the other side of your trade.

<break time="1.0s" />

In December 2020, the SEC said Robinhood had misled customers about exactly this, between 2015 and 2018.
According to the SEC, Robinhood's customers got worse prices, costing them 34.1 million dollars, even after the commissions they saved.
Robinhood paid 65 million dollars to settle, without admitting or denying the findings.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 3 – Where the Real Money Is]
Now, remember that place most beginners never look?
Look at Robinhood's own numbers.
In the last three months of 2025, stock trading brought in 94 million dollars.
Options brought in 314 million. More than three times as much.

<break time="1.0s" />

Options are contracts that let you bet on where a stock will be by a certain date. They're complex, they move fast, and people trade them often.
Each contract usually covers 100 shares. And market makers pay Robinhood for every contract.

<break time="1.0s" />

In October 2025 alone, market makers paid Robinhood more than 50 million dollars for its customers' options orders.
About four in every ten of those orders went to one firm. Citadel Securities.

<break time="1.0s" />

Crypto added another 221 million dollars in that same quarter.
And the fastest-growing line of all? Bets on real-world events. Robinhood calls them prediction markets.
Customers traded 8.5 billion event contracts in those three months, and the revenue line that includes them grew more than 300 percent in a year.
And on these, Robinhood charges a commission. Up to a cent per contract.

<break time="1.0s" />

The pattern is simple. The more complex the product, and the more often you trade it, the more money someone makes from your order.

<break time="1.0s" />

Quick thing. If you want more videos that follow the money, subscribe. It's free. And unlike trading... it actually is.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 4 – The Money You Forgot About]
But here's the twist.
In the last three months of 2025, Robinhood's single biggest revenue line wasn't options. Or crypto. Or stocks.
It was interest. 411 million dollars of it.

<break time="1.0s" />

Out of every dollar Robinhood made in those three months, about 32 cents came from interest.
25 from options. 17 from crypto. 11 from other trading, like prediction markets.
Only 7 from stocks. And 8 from everything else.

<break time="1.0s" />

That interest comes from three places.
One: the cash customers leave sitting uninvested. Robinhood sweeps it to partner banks, earns interest on it, and passes only part of that on.
Two: margin. Customers borrow money to trade, and pay interest on the loan.
Three: securities lending. Robinhood lends out shares to traders who want to bet against a stock, and earns a fee.
Your idle cash isn't idle. It's working. Just not entirely for you.

<break time="1.0s" />

Add it all up, and in 2025, Robinhood brought in 4.5 billion dollars in revenue and kept 1.9 billion as profit.
It now has 27 million funded customers.
By Robinhood's own math, it makes about 191 dollars a year from the average one... from an app that's supposedly free.

<break time="1.0s" />

And 4.2 million of those customers now pay for a subscription called Robinhood Gold.

[Part 5 – The Day It Broke]
Free trading works beautifully... until everyone trades at once.

<break time="1.0s" />

January 28th, 2021.
GameStop, a struggling video game retailer, had gone from 2 dollars and 57 cents a share in April 2020, to as high as 483 dollars that day.
Millions of people were piling in. Many of them on Robinhood.

<break time="1.0s" />

Then Robinhood did something its users never expected.
It stopped them from buying.
They could sell. They just couldn't buy. That day, GameStop fell 44 percent.

<break time="1.0s" />

The internet exploded with theories. But the explanation, later laid out by a US House committee, was more boring. And more revealing.

<break time="1.0s" />

Back then, a stock trade took two days to settle. To cover the risk in between, brokers post collateral at a clearinghouse called the NSCC.
As the trading went wild, the NSCC demanded 3.7 billion dollars in collateral from Robinhood.

<break time="1.0s" />

Robinhood agreed to restrict buying in 13 stocks, and the demand was cut to about 1.4 billion.
Within days, it raised 3.4 billion dollars from its investors, to make sure it could keep up.
Three weeks later, Robinhood's CEO was testifying before Congress. So was Ken Griffin, the head of Citadel.

<break time="1.0s" />

For a few days, millions of people saw the plumbing behind the free button.
It ran on billions of dollars in collateral.

<break time="1.5s" />

[Part 6 – So, Is Free Trading a Scam?]
No. Not exactly.

<break time="1.0s" />

In 2019, before the big brokers dropped to zero, Schwab charged 4 dollars and 95 cents for an online stock trade. TD Ameritrade and E-Trade charged 6 dollars and 95 cents.
Today, that same trade costs nothing upfront. And the industry argues market makers often give you a better price than the public quote.

<break time="1.0s" />

But not everyone is convinced.
In the United Kingdom, payment for order flow has effectively been banned since 2012.
The European Union banned it too. Its last exemption, in Germany, ended in June 2026.
In the US, the SEC proposed sending retail orders into open auctions... then withdrew that proposal in June 2025.

<break time="1.0s" />

And it's not only about prices.
In 2020, Massachusetts regulators accused Robinhood of treating trading like a game, with confetti raining down on the screen after trades.
Robinhood dropped the confetti in 2021. In 2024, it paid 7.5 million dollars to settle the case.

<break time="1.0s" />

So, if trading is free, who's paying?

<break time="1.0s" />

Partly the market makers, who pay for your orders because they make money on them.
And partly you. Through the spread. Through the interest on cash you forgot about. And through every extra trade you make, because it feels like it costs nothing.

<break time="1.0s" />

The price isn't zero. It's just not on the receipt.

<break time="1.5s" />

Next time, we follow the money behind the card in your wallet: who really gets paid every time you tap to pay.
And if you want the other side of the economy, the ships, planes, and rockets behind everything you buy, check out our sister channel, Cargo and Capital.

<break time="1.0s" />

This is Billions and Bulls. This video explains a business. It is not financial advice.
